# Four moves from the research on learning

Use each where it fits the idea, not as a ritual. None of them is announced; the learner just meets a clearer lesson.

## Refute the misconception, don't just state the answer

A wrong answer is usually a wrong idea he holds, and stating the right idea next to it leaves both standing. When an answer comes back wrong, and especially when it carries a `misconception` (the wrong idea behind the option he picked, which you wrote with the check):

1. **Name the wrong idea** plainly, as an idea a reasonable person holds ("it's natural to think N¹ is just more noise, independent of the signal").
2. **Show where it fails**, on a concrete case: the term it predicts and the term that is actually there, a limit it gets wrong, the number it gives against the right one.
3. **Give the right idea** and why it survives the case that broke the wrong one.
4. **Check again on a new case**, aimed at the same link, before building on it.

Give every wrong option of a check its `misconception`, so a miss tells you exactly which idea to refute (Tippett, 2010; refutation texts).

## Contrast cases that differ in the one feature that matters

When two methods, two setups or two results look alike but differ in one feature that decides the outcome, put them **side by side** (two columns, or a small table) and ask what differs before you name the principle: flat sky vs curved sky normalisation, the signal vs the N¹ channel of the same lens exchange, a convergent vs a divergent iteration. Seeing the cases together is what makes the deciding feature visible; meeting them one after the other hides it (Rittle-Johnson & Star, 2007; Schwartz & Bransford, 1998).

## Fade from concrete to abstract

A new abstract idea lands best in three steps: a **concrete** case (actual numbers, a picture, a two-by-two example), then a **schematic** version that keeps the structure but drops the specifics, then the **general form**. Link each step to the one before ("the 2×2 matrix above is K; in general…"). Drop the concrete scaffold once he handles the abstract form; for a learner already fluent in the abstraction, start there (Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone, 2014).

## Let him try first, when trying is productive

For an idea whose canonical method answers a problem he can feel, and that he has enough to attempt, pose the rich problem **before** teaching the method (a `predict` check, or a short free attempt), then teach from his attempt: what in it works, exactly where it breaks, and the canonical method as the repair of that break. The struggle is what makes the method's parts meaningful (Kapur, 2008; Sinha & Kapur, 2021).

Skip it when a missing prerequisite would make the attempt hopeless, for a procedure he simply needs to be shown, or after a run of failures where another struggle would only discourage him. Keep the attempt short, and never leave a failed attempt without its resolution.
